### ALE-007 · fine · 124w
>>>
Why a few words and not a question? A chat engine seems to answer questions, but underneath it only ever <i>continues text</i>. Behind the scenes, the conversation is written out like a script — <i>Operator: … Engine: —</i> — and the machine carries the script on from where it stops. The next thing in the script is always the engine’s reply, so it looks like an answer. The rules for how it should behave (the <i>system prompt</i>) are just more text, put in front of yours. This cabinet skips the script and asks for opening words directly. The punch above the slot is real history, too: cards like these were cut one column at a time on a hand punch. Someone always has to turn words into holes.

### ALE-024 · truth · 134w

<span class="lead">In truth —</span> attention plus a small calculator makes one <b>layer</b>. Layers are stacked — dozens of them, close to a hundred in a big model. A word’s numbers are changed a little at every floor. Low floors seem to handle spelling and grammar; higher floors handle meaning and intent — or so the people who study these machines report. The adjustable parts are called <b>parameters</b>. A big model has hundreds of billions of them, and not one was set by hand. In <b>training</b>, the engine read a huge library and guessed each next word. Every time it guessed wrong, every screw was turned a hair. Do that a great many times and that is the whole of its schooling. A finishing school comes after — training on what people prefer — to make it helpful, honest, and polite.

A modern LLM produces its probabilities not by counting but by the learned machinery of Stations III–V, and its scale is of another order: a catalogue of ~50,000–200,000 tokens against our <span id="vN2">—</span> · a rack of ~200,000 slugs against our 24 · ~100 floors against our pretended 6 · a thousand milliard parameters against our <span id="vK2">—</span> wheels. ### ALE-041 · truth · 123w
>>>
<span class="lead">Post-scriptum, on the question of building it.</span> A Large Analytical Language Engine of our acquaintance has computed what a full-sized specimen would come to, wrought honestly in brass at one counting-column to each parameter. At a million million parts, and with decent aisles between machines for the oilers to labour, the works would cover an area approximately the size of Wales. With every mill labouring together at Mr.&nbsp;Babbage’s own pace, it might mint one word in a season, and a full correspondence in a generation. Its boilers would want half the coal of Britain, and its frames would require more brass than the world of 1843 had ever smelted. The arithmetic is left in the margin for the sceptical.
<<<

### ALE-042 · fine · 78w
>>>
Marginalia — <b>area:</b> 10¹² parameters × one five-wheel column at a hand’s-breadth square, (0.1&nbsp;m)², ≈ 10,000&nbsp;km²; doubled for aisles and shafting ≈ Wales, 20,779&nbsp;km². <b>Pace:</b> ~2×10¹² multiply-accumulations the token, shared among two million mills at ten seconds the operation ≈ four months the word. <b>Coal:</b> those mills steam-driven Victorian efficiency ≈ tens of millions of tons the year, Britain’s whole output of 1850 being about sixty million. <b>Brass:</b> ~½&nbsp;kg the column ≈ 500 million tons — centuries of smelting, as reckoned from 1843.
<<<

### ALE-043 · truth · 129w
>>>
<span class="lead">Post-post-scriptum, on the labour question.</span> A correspondent of sterner economy, a certain Mr.&nbsp;E.&nbsp;Scrooge, London, asks if the treadwheel might replace the boiler, with the destitute and the orphan turning the works while saving the expense of burning coal. Prisons and workhouses are amply stocked with labour. The arithmetic does not support his conjectures: to replace the boilers would want some forty million souls in relays — half again the population of Great Britain — upon eight hundred and fifty thousand wheels; every pauper actually on the parish books, treading day and night, would hasten each four-month word by merely four days; and the orphans at their cranks would buy, at most, an hour. Bread, moreover, runs thirty times dearer than coal, watt for watt.
<<<

### ALE-044 · fine · 116w
>>>
Marginalia — <b>demand:</b> ~1&nbsp;GW at the prime movers, as computed above. <b>A treader:</b> ~60&nbsp;W for a ten-hour day; a child at the crank, ~15&nbsp;W. <b>Replacement:</b> 10⁹&nbsp;÷&nbsp;60 ≈ 17 million treading at every hour, ×2.4 for relays ≈ 40 million; ÷&nbsp;20 places the wheel ≈ 850,000 wheels, whose sheds ≈ 42&nbsp;km² — a five-hundredth part of Wales, the scheme’s one economy. <b>As found:</b> Britain’s ~1.5 million paupers yield ~37&nbsp;MW ≈ 4 per cent ≈ four days the word; its ~65,000 workhouse orphans ~0.4&nbsp;MW ≈ an hour. <b>The ledger:</b> a pauper’s keep, ~5d the day, buys ~2&nbsp;MJ of work; a ton of coal at 10s buys 1.5&nbsp;GJ — near thirty-fold the value. The penal wheel was struck off by the Prison Act of 1898.

### BARD-050 · truth · 186w
>>>
<span class="lead">In truth —</span> nothing new can come out. Every line is a walk through the sonnets the machine has read, joining them at words Shakespeare happened to use twice.<br>
      The engine read the poems line by line, as they were printed in 1609, so it has learned roughly how long a line runs and where one ends — it even has a mark of its own for the ending, <b>⏎</b>. What makes the whole <i>sheet</i> look like a sonnet is not the engine but the <b>harness</b>: the row of cams above it, which supplies the fourteen lines and the rhyme scheme.<br>
      Each cam is one rule laid on the lottery. It either strikes words out of the drum before the draw, or tilts the odds toward the ones it wants. (The trade calls this <b>constrained decoding</b>.) A cam can forbid and it can steer — it cannot invent. Only the counting-wheels ever propose a word. If the engine has never read a rhyme for <i>compass</i>, no rule can produce one, and the margin will say so with a ✗. Watch for that failure: it is the whole point of the exhibit.
<<<

The trade calls this whole panel <i>the harness</i>, and every modern engine has one. When a chat machine refuses a question, or answers in a tidy list, or knows to stop talking — that is mostly harness, not intelligence.<br>
      Two things are worth taking away. First: <b>the harness can shape what the engine says, but it cannot add anything the engine does not know.</b> Second: <b>what the harness can achieve depends entirely on how much the engine has read.</b> Throw the Rhyme Cam at fifteen sonnets, then at all 154, and count the ✗ marks for yourself.<br>
      Rhyme is judged as Shakespeare judged it — by sound, allowing his own old pronunciations (<i>love</i> with <i>prove</i>, <i>eyes</i> with <i>lies</i>), which are marked in the margin as such. A word never rhymes with itself. And like him, the press does not stop for an imperfect rhyme.<br>
      The press runs on the same boiler as the crank — the lever at Station VII — so your sheet is printed at whatever setting stands there.<br>
      When the sheet is full, the <b>concordance</b> beneath it underlines every run of three or more words that appears, in that order, somewhere in the sonnets, and names the line it came from. It then tallies how much of the sheet is quotation and how much is the machine's own stitching. That is the question every reader of any engine's output ought to ask — and here it can be answered exactly, because everything the machine has ever read is on this page.

<span class="lead">On fine-tuning, which is what this is.</span> The trade calls a machine re-schooled on a narrower library a <b>fine-tune</b>, and this cabinet is one in the strictest sense: identical architecture, identical mechanism, only the reading exchanged. Observe that the voice came along with the reading. A style, to the machine, is nothing but a set of probabilities — and the more distinctive the style, the sharper the probabilities and the easier the counterfeit. Observe too where the counterfeit’s seams fall: every splice we have caught turns on a word the poet <i>himself</i> repeated — <i>fair</i> from <i>fair</i>, <i>eternal</i> summer and <i>eternal</i> lines, <i>love</i> upon <i>love</i>. His rhetorical figures are, quite literally, the graph’s hubs. The style is the topology.

<span class="lead">On the schooling lever, and what it teaches.</span> The five notches are the same machine, taught from more or less Shakespeare: fifteen sonnets, forty, eighty, a hundred and fifteen, or all one hundred and fifty-four. The registers on the desk show what that costs — <b>sorts</b> is how many different words it knows, <b>wheels</b> is how many counts it holds. Both climb steadily: 738 sorts at fifteen sonnets, 1,443 at forty, 2,168 at eighty, 2,666 at a hundred and fifteen, 3,216 at the whole.<br>
Each notch teaches it words it could not say before. Try these cards, and watch the same seed answered differently as you move the lever:<br>
<i>“not marble, nor the gilded”</i> — nothing at fifteen sonnets; <i>monuments</i>, nine times in ten, from forty.<br>
<i>“make war upon this bloody”</i> — <i>tyrant</i> arrives at eighty.<br>
<i>“wherefore with infection should he”</i> — <i>live</i> arrives at a hundred and fifteen.<br>
<i>“two loves i have of comfort and”</i> — <i>despair</i> waits for all one hundred and fifty-four. This last is the interesting one: at a hundred and fifteen the engine already knows the word <i>despair</i>, but has never seen it follow <i>and</i>, so it cannot get there. Knowing a word and knowing where it goes are two different things. At the top notch the sheets change character. They quote just as much, but in shorter stretches, stitched together more often — the engine has more roads to choose from, so it takes more turnings. That is what training data really is: not cleverness, but coverage.

<span class="lead">On the press and its rule-book.</span> The rule-book is the work of Prof. J. G. Rashbold-Wordsmith of Baillie College, Oxford, who studied the quarto of 1609 and satisfied himself that he had worked out how Shakespeare writes: that the manner of the sonnets — the pointing, the measure, the rhyme, the turn, the grammar, the keeping to a subject — comes down to a finite set of habits, and that a machine given the habits as rules, and the words as counting-wheels, would write as he did. Each cam above is one of his habits, cut in brass. The press writes fourteen lines because his rule-book makes it, not because the engine has any idea what a sonnet is.<br>
The rule-book — the <b>harness</b> — can forbid and it can steer, but it cannot invent. It may strike a full stop out of the lottery, or tilt the odds toward a rhyming word. But if the engine has never read a rhyme for <i>compass</i>, no rule can conjure one, and the margin admits it with a ✗. Much of a modern chat machine's good behaviour works exactly this way.<br>
The <b>Concord Cam</b> shows the limit best. It lays a rule of grammar on the lottery: no two articles in a row, an article wants its noun, an auxiliary wants its verb, <i>i am</i> and <i>thou art</i> rather than <i>i art</i>. In its second edition it reads two more tables off the same wheels: each noun’s number, from the little words the sonnets set before it, and whether each verb takes an object; so a plural subject may not have a singular verb, and a verb that wants its object may not end the line. It can forbid every shape that could never parse — and a line may still pass it and mean nothing whatever. Grammar is not sense.<br><span class="lead">The Professor’s finding, which he did not intend.</span> Every rule in his book is true of Shakespeare. Every sheet the press prints satisfies every rule he cut. And no sheet is Shakespeare. He set out to show that a manner is a set of habits; the machine shows instead that the habits are what is left of a manner when the meaning is taken away. That is the whole finding, and it is the reason the press is kept running.<br>
The <b>Subject Cam</b> is the one nearest to what a modern engine does with your question. The card names the subject. The engine already keeps a book of which words have stood in a verse line together — the same book the Attention station reads — and this cam tilts every draw toward the subject's company in that book, so the poem is held to its matter instead of drifting wherever the last two words lead. Maillardet's automaton kept its poems on their subject the same way, by cams cut for each poem; ours is cut afresh from the card. It can hold the poem to a subject; it cannot know what a subject is, and it cannot supply company the sonnets never gave. If the card names no word the wheels have read, it idles, and the tally says so.<br>
Nor are its guesses infallible. It works out each word's part of speech from the company that word keeps in the sonnets, and it gets some wrong: <i>love</i> is both noun and verb, and <i>shines</i> it takes for a thing, having once seen it follow <i>heaven</i>. The tally beneath the sheet reports what it struck, so you can judge it yourself.

Marginalia — the small print, plainly.<br>
<b>There is one boiler.</b> It is the lever at Station VII, and it drives the press as well as the crank. Whatever setting stands there is the setting your sheet is printed at. Turn it down and the engine quotes more and wanders less; turn it up and it takes wilder turns. Nothing is hidden in the press.<br>
<b>Why 0.95?</b> We tried every setting from 0.70 to 1.30, three hundred times each, and counted two things: how much of the output was real Shakespeare, and how much was new rather than copied out whole. Anywhere from 0.70 to 1.05 scores much the same. Past about 1.1 it falls apart. We left the lever at 0.95; for printing sonnets, 0.85 quotes a little more. Try both.<br>
<b>How rhyme is judged.</b> The rhyme scheme of a sonnet is no secret, so the sonnets tell us which words Shakespeare rhymed: 1,060 pairs of them. Our rhymer sounds out spelling, and gets about six in seven right that way. The rest — <i>love</i> with <i>prove</i>, <i>done</i> with <i>sun</i>, <i>die</i> with <i>memory</i> — it simply learns from him, and only from the sonnets it has been given. A word never rhymes with itself, nor with its own possessive. <i>Beauty</i> and <i>memory</i> chime, but faintly; we mark them as his licence, not true rhyme.<br>
<b>How the Rhyme Cam works.</b> It picks the line's last word first, from the words Shakespeare ends lines with, then steers the line toward it. When it fails, it fails honestly: a ✗ means the engine knows no path to any rhyming word, and it knows that before the line begins. Roughly three lines in four find their rhyme — at the smallest schooling and the largest alike.<br>
<b>How the Subject Cam works.</b> The card names the subject. The engine keeps a book of which words have stood in a verse line together (the Attention station reads the same book), and the cam gives the subject’s company a share of the drum — up to a fifth, more the more company the sonnets gave it. Measured over five cards: a subject the sonnets surround richly (<i>summer’s day</i>, <i>mistress’ eyes</i>, <i>love</i>) has ten to sixteen more of every hundred full words keeping its company, at a price of three to nine points of quotation; a subject they scarcely mention is barely moved — the cam has nothing to hold it with. Turn it up and the poem holds tighter and quotes less; there is no free setting.<br>
