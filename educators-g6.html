Thread 1 The Machine

Introduce the pipeline as input → process → output, and the engine as an algorithm — a recipe followed exactly, with no judgment calls. New precision for this age: words become numbered tokens (Station II shows each word's catalogue number); the machine's memory rack holds only 24 of them — its context window — so old words fall off and are forgotten; and the output loops back into the input (Station VIII), which is why it writes one word at a time. The phone-keyboard autocomplete comparison works here: same idea, smaller machine.

Thread 2 The Mathematics

The gauges are now read as percentages that sum to 100: a probability distribution, though you needn't use the phrase yet. The big idea is weighted chance: the lottery drum gives every candidate a slice of the wheel proportional to its gauge. Run the same prompt several times: different answers from identical starting conditions, because a fair draw was made from the same odds. The boiler lever makes this quantitative: cold sharpens the odds toward the favourite (repetitive), hot flattens them (surprising). This is a genuine variability-and-distributions lesson with a brass interface.

Thread 3 The True History

Fill in the real machines. The Difference Engine (designed from the 1820s) had one job: compute and print mathematical tables without human error — a serious matter when ships navigated by printed numbers. The government funded it lavishly but it was never completed. The Analytical Engine (from 1834) was a leap forward: a general machine with a Mill that calculates and a Store that remembers — recognisably a CPU and memory — programmed by punched cards borrowed from the Jacquard loom, which wove silk pictures from thousands of cards. Ada Lovelace translated a paper about the engine and tripled its length with her own Notes, including a card-by-card procedure for computing a famous number sequence — often called the first published computer program. In 1991 London's Science Museum finished a Difference Engine from Babbage's drawings: it weighs five tonnes, has thousands of parts, and calculates flawlessly.

Thread 4 Can a Machine Think?

"The Analytical Engine has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform."— Ada Lovelace, Note G (1843)

Now put her claim under mild pressure: the engine just drew a word that surprised the whole class. Is surprising us the same as originating something? Where did the surprise actually come from: the machine, the “dice” (temperature), or the 79 sentences it read? Debate in pairs; there are good answers on both sides, and students should discover that "it's just following orders" and "it surprised me" can both be true at once.

Thread 5 At the Cabinet

  • The temperature experiment. Same prompt, five runs at FROST, five at FEVERISH. Record each output; count how many distinct sentences each setting produced. Graph it. Conclusion in the students' own words: what does the lever really control?
  • The forgetting game. One writer may see only the last three words of the class story before adding one. How does a short memory change what gets written? Connect to the 24-slug rack.
  • Type Drawer → By casting. Why is “the” box biggest? The compartments are sized by how often each word appears — a first meeting with the deeply lopsided way language uses its words.
  • A paper engine. Tally the word-pairs of one nursery rhyme, then generate a "new" line by rolling dice over the tallies. The class has now built the machine's heart with paper and a pencil.
