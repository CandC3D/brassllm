The Analytical Language Engine

For Educators

one brass machine, four grade levels — computer science, mathematics, history, and the question of whether a machine can think

The Analytical Language Engine is a working miniature of a large language model, dressed as a Victorian brass machine. Its probabilities are computed live; nothing is staged. That makes it usable as a genuine laboratory instrument — the same cabinet serves an eight-year-old playing a guessing game and a senior seminar on the philosophy of mind.

How this guide works

This is a spiral curriculum: the same five threads return at each grade band, each time with more sophistication. Nothing taught early has to be untaught later — the machine a third grader meets as a "guessing machine" is the same machine a twelfth grader analyses as an interpolated Markov model, and both descriptions are true. Each grade has its own page; share the one your colleagues need.

Ten-minute orientation (for you, before class)

  • Open the Engine. Leave Wait at each station ticked; pull Begin the Demonstration and press Proceed (or Enter) through all eight stations once.
  • Afterwards, Turn the Crank a few times and Run the Engine once to the stop mark ∎.
  • Move the boiler lever (Station VII) and watch the gauges at Station VI re-compute instantly — this is your best single demonstration.
  • Open the Type Drawer under Station II — the machine's entire vocabulary, all ~231 words.
  • Optional: tick Let the works be heard for sound. For the arithmetic itself, read The Mathematics Behind the Engine.
A note on honesty. The engine's plaques distinguish what is genuinely computed (tokenisation, the probabilities, temperature, the lottery) from what is honestly illustrative (the embedding dials, the attention linkages, the mill's floors). Teaching that distinction is itself a lesson in how to talk about AI — model it for students rather than smoothing it over.

Primary sources (all freely available online)

  • L. F. Menabrea, Sketch of the Analytical Engine (1842), translated with Notes by Ada Lovelace (1843) — Notes A and G are the classroom essentials.
  • A. M. Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," Mind 59 (1950) — answers "Lady Lovelace's Objection" by name.
  • J. R. Searle, "Minds, Brains, and Programs," Behavioral and Brain Sciences 3 (1980).
  • E. M. Bender et al., "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots," FAccT (2021).
  • The Science Museum, London — Difference Engine No. 2, built 1985–1991, printer completed 2002.

Standards touchpoints

Offered as orientation, not a formal crosswalk:

  • Grade 3: CCSS Math 3.MD (represent and interpret data); operations & patterns (3.OA); collaborative discussion (ELA SL.3.1).
  • Grade 6: CCSS 6.SP (distributions and variability), previewing 7.SP.C (probability models — formally a Grade 7 domain, met here through experiment); CSTA algorithms & data.
  • Grade 9: CCSS HS S-CP (conditional probability), S-IC (inference from randomised experiment); CSTA impacts of computing & AI.
  • Grade 12: S-MD (probability in decisions); AP CS Principles big ideas (algorithms, data, impact); seminar practice in philosophy and history of science.

Every number this guide cites about the cabinet — 79 sentences, ~231 sorts, 24 rack places, the λ weights — is live in the machine and checkable by your students. If they catch a discrepancy, they have learned the most important lesson of all: audit the machine.
