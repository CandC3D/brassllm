Being a candid mechanical account of the Large Language Model — rendered in brass, in the manner of Mr. Charles Babbage, and schooled upon nothing whatever but the Sonnets of Mr. William Shakespeare.

Everything below actually runs: the gauges are moved by a real (if miniature) engine of probability computed live on this page. The plaques state, candidly, how the full-sized article differs — and terms under a dotted line yield their modern meaning to the pointer.

Station I

The Card Writer

Your words are punched upon a card at the clavier — a stroke to the column — and fed to the reader. The engine does not hear you — it receives marks.

In truth — a language model receives nothing but text: no voice, no view of the world, no notion of who is asking beyond what the words themselves carry. Everything that follows is determined by these characters and by the settings of the machine’s parts.

A note on the manner of address. To a chat engine you write whole questions; this cabinet asks instead for a few opening words to continue. There is no contradiction — a chat engine is a continuation engine too: the clerks lay the conversation out as a transcript (Operator: … Engine: —) and the machine simply continues the document, which lands, every time, precisely upon the engine’s reply. The instructions that govern its manners — the system prompt — are more cards still, read before yours. The clavier above the slot is no fancy, either: real cards were cut at just such a hand-punch, a stroke to the column — somebody must first turn words into holes.

Station II

The Type-Sorting Works

Fig. 2 — tokenisation
The pin-reader · a pin passes where a hole permits · a finished word releases its slug
The reader stands ready; feed it a card.
The composed line · every slug bears its catalogue number
rack empty — 0 of 24 places filled

The card is read and matched to slugs — standard pieces from the engine’s type-case. Each slug bears a catalogue number, and from here onward the engine handles numbers only.

In truth — text is broken into tokens from a fixed catalogue of roughly 50,000–200,000 pieces. Common words are one token; rarer words are cut into fragments (un·fathom·able). The machine never sees letters or words as you do — only these numbers.

This cabinet’s catalogue holds — slugs; a slug marked ✶ was cut fresh and is a stranger to it. The rack holds only 24 slugs — its context window. Push in more and the oldest fall off the far end, forgotten. (A modern engine’s rack holds 200,000 or more.)

Pick up a sort to read its particulars.

In truth — this drawer is the model’s entire vocabulary. Every reply it will ever compose is set from these sorts and no others; a word absent from the drawer cannot be said. A real engine’s case holds 50,000–200,000 sorts — larger, but every bit as closed. Sorts marked ✶ were cut fresh this session; sorts aglow are out at the composing line.

Station III

The Ledger of Meanings

Fig. 3 — embeddings
Specimens from the ledger · a fixed exhibit
Note how LOVE and EYES set their dials near one another, while SUMMER wears a different aspect.
The slugs at hand, exchanged for dial-columns
— awaiting slugs —

Each catalogue number is looked up in the Great Ledger, and the slug is exchanged for a column of dial-settings — its position upon the map of meaning.

In truth — every token becomes an embedding: a list of thousands of numbers (this cabinet shows eight dials; a large engine uses more than ten thousand). Nobody wrote the ledger by hand — the settings were learned, and they place words used in similar ways at similar settings. Meaning, to the machine, is position.

Station IV

The Linkage of Regards

Fig. 4 — attention
Two banks of linkages · thickness is regard
— awaiting slugs —
the RECENCY head (above)the AFFINITY head (below)

Here is the celebrated mechanism. Every slug is joined to every earlier slug by an adjustable linkage; the engine draws some taut and lets others hang slack. A word discovers what it means by choosing what to regard.

In truth — this is attention, the invention (2017) that makes these engines possible. For each position the model computes how much every earlier token should bear upon it — how it finds its noun, how bank leans toward the river or the counting-house. Dozens of heads work in parallel, each minding a different sort of relation: shown here, a nickel head that favours the recent, a copper head that favours old acquaintances.

Depicted schematically — in the full engine these weights are computed afresh, at every layer, from the dial-settings themselves.

Station V

The Grand Mill

Fig. 5 — layers & training
Cross-section · six floors of a hundred · the bars trace one column’s settings
The mill stands ready.

The columns now descend through the Mill — floor after identical floor of linkage-and-gearwork, each refining the settings handed down by the last.

In truth — attention plus a small calculating network make one layer, and layers are stacked by the dozen (a large model, near a hundred). A token’s numbers are revised at every floor — coarse matters of spelling and grammar low down, subtler matters of sense and intent above, or so the anatomists report. The adjustable parts — the parameters — number in the hundreds of billions, and none was set by hand: in training, the engine read a vast library, guessed each next token, and every screw was turned a hair’s-breadth whenever it guessed wrong. Repeated a very great many times, that is the whole of its education. A finishing school follows — training on human preference — to make it helpful, honest, and polite.

This cabinet, for comparison, owns — counting-wheels, learned from a library of — verse lines.

Station VI

The Manometer Wall

Fig. 6 — the probability board
Live gauges · the seven strongest candidates, and the rest pooled
— the gauges stand at rest —

At the mill’s outfall the engine renders its judgment — not a word, but a pressure upon every gauge in the house: a score for every slug in the catalogue at once.

In truth — the model’s output at each step is a probability for every token in its vocabulary — all 50,000-odd, most of them minuscule. It does not decide the next word; it rates all possible ones. These gauges are live: they show this engine’s true arithmetic for the present context — and they obey the boiler lever at Station VII, should you care to move it.

One courtesy, disclosed: for the first two words after a fresh card the stop-mark and the full stops are held at nought and their pressure returned to the rest, so that a card of a single word cannot be answered with silence. From the third word the engine is naked, and may rest when it likes.

Station VII

The Governor & the Lottery Drum

Fig. 7 — temperature & sampling
the governor — slack when cold, flung wide by heat
WARM · T = 0.95
The lottery drum · lots cast in proportion to the gauges
No lot has yet been drawn.

One slug must be drawn. The lots are cast in proportion to the gauges — and the boiler decides how strictly. Run cold, the favourite is all but certain; run feverish, and outsiders take their chance.

In truth — the next token is chosen by weighted lottery — sampling — and temperature reshapes the odds exactly as shown: low temperature sharpens the distribution toward the favourite (predictable, sometimes repetitious); high temperature flattens it (inventive, and at the extreme, incoherent). This is why the same question, asked twice, may be answered differently. Try the lever — the gauges obey at once.

Station VIII

The Endless Crank

Fig. 8 — the loop, and stopping
The tape · and the crank that turns it all again
❧
REVOLUTION № 000
blue ink — your card · dark ink — minted by the engine · ∎ — the stop token

The chosen slug is stamped upon the tape — and carried straight back to the rack, where it joins your question. The whole engine then turns over again, from card to lottery, to choose the word after. One revolution, one word.

In truth — a language model writes autoregressively: one token per pass, each pass re-reading all that came before. There is no finished answer waiting inside; each word is minted the moment before you read it. And the engine stops as it speaks — by predicting a special stop token (printed here as ∎), whereupon it rests.

The full engine keeps notes — a cache — so as not to redo old arithmetic on every revolution; but in principle, every turn reads the whole rack afresh.

The Sonnet Press

The concordance · which of the press’s words stand in the sonnets, in order · quoted runs underscored
a run is
The press stands ready; your card seeds the first line.

Designed and built in a Victorian burst of enthusiasm for Shakespeare, the press sets the engine running line upon line — fourteen to the sheet, quatrains and couplet — in the hope, sincerely held and entirely vain, that new poetic masterpieces might emerge.

In truth — nothing can emerge but recombination. Every line is a walk through the sonnets the machine has read, spliced at the words Mr. Shakespeare himself repeated. The engine has read the poems as the quarto set them — line by line — so the shape of a single line it proposes for itself, down to the mark ⏎ by which it ends one; what makes the sheet look like a sonnet is the harness — the rule-book of cams above it, which owns the fourteen-line frame and the rhyme scheme. Each cam is a rule laid upon the lottery: it strikes forbidden slugs from the drum before the draw (the trade calls this constrained decoding), or steers the draw toward permitted ones. The cams forbid and steer; they cannot invent. Only the counting-wheels propose a word — and if the engine has never read a rhyme for compass, no rule can conjure one. Watch the Rhyme Cam fail, and mark it ✗ in the margin: that failure is the exhibit.

The trade term for the whole panel is the harness, and it is no small part of any modern engine: the good manners of a chat machine — its refusals, its formats, its stopping when it should — are largely rule-book rather than model. Two lessons follow. The harness cannot add knowledge, only shape it; and what it can do is bounded by what the engine has read — throw the Rhyme Cam at five sonnets, then at the full folio, and count the ✗ marks: the failures thin as the library grows, though not by a straight road. Rhyme is judged as the Bard would: by sound, with his own eye-rhymes and old pronunciations allowed (love/prove, eyes/lies) and marked in the margin as such — never a word against itself, nor against its own possessive; and, like him, the press does not stop for an imperfect rhyme. The press runs at its own boiler setting, 0.85, cooler than the crank's — of which more in the marginalia below. When the sheet is full the concordance beneath it underscores every run of three or more words that stands, in order, in the sonnets — a slip names the source line — and tallies how much of the sheet is quotation and how much is seam. It is the one question every reader of an engine's output should ask, and here it can be answered exactly, because the whole of what the machine has read is on the page.

A candid note, screwed to the cabinet.

This is the same machine, differently schooled. The Analytical Bard Engine is the Analytical Language Engine in every brass particular — the same clavier, mill, gauges, governor, drum and crank, and the same miniature counting engine (a Markov chain) beneath them. What differs is only what it has read: not the parent’s little library of household prose, but — verse lines from the Sonnets of Mr. William Shakespeare, read as the quarto set them, presently — sorts in the catalogue and — counting-wheels. Its every word is his; the order is the lottery’s. For the candid account of how such an engine differs from the full-sized article — its scale, its arithmetic, and the celebrated question of whether one might be built in brass across Wales — the reader is referred to the parent cabinet, whose notes we do not repeat here.

On fine-tuning, which is what this is. The trade calls a machine re-schooled on a narrower library a fine-tune, and this cabinet is one in the strictest sense: identical architecture, identical mechanism, only the reading exchanged. Observe that the voice came along with the reading. A style, to the machine, is nothing but a set of probabilities — and the more distinctive the style, the sharper the probabilities and the easier the counterfeit. Observe too where the counterfeit’s seams fall: every splice we have caught turns on a word the poet himself repeated — fair from fair, eternal summer and eternal lines, love upon love. His rhetorical figures are, quite literally, the graph’s hubs. The style is the topology.

On the schooling lever, and what it teaches. The seven notches are the same machine at seven sizes of library — five sonnets up to the whole sequence of one hundred and fifty-four — and the registers on the desk report the consequence in sorts and wheels — the two quantities the trade calls vocabulary and parameters. Seed the card with “when i do count the clock” at five sonnets and the engine can only stammer, for it has never read the twelfth; at ten it continues “that tells the time” at better than nine parts in ten. Seed “not marble, nor the gilded” and only the twentieth-sonnet folio knows monuments; seed “two loves i have of comfort and” and only the whole sequence knows despair. At the top notch — 3,216 sorts, some 36,500 wheels — the sheets change character: they quote as much as ever, but in shorter runs, more often spliced, for the engine has more roads and takes more turns. That is what training data is: not cleverness, but coverage.

On the press and its rule-book. The sonnet press was built in a burst of enthusiasm and in the vain hope of masterpieces; it composes fourteen lines because the rule-book compels it, not because the engine knows what a sonnet is. The rule-book — the harness — forbids and steers but cannot invent: it can strike a full stop from the drum, or tilt the lottery toward a rhyme, but if the wheels have never read a rhyme for compass, no cam can conjure one, and the margin says so with a ✗. Two lessons are engraved there. The harness cannot add knowledge, only shape it; and its success rises with the schooling — throw the Rhyme Cam at five sonnets and again at twenty, and count the marks. Much of a modern chat engine’s good behaviour is harness of exactly this kind. The Concord Cam is the most telling of all: it lays a rule of grammar on the drum — no two of a kind, an article wants its noun, an auxiliary its verb, i am and thou art — and it can strike every shape that could never parse; yet a line that passes it may still mean nothing. Its guesses at each slug’s part are made from the company the slug keeps in the sonnets, and they err (love is noun and verb; shines is taken for a thing because it once followed heaven) — which the margin, and the tally, will show.

Marginalia — the boiler carries two settings, each by measurement, not taste, and each belonging to its own lever. The crank's 0.95: three hundred free completions of the seed shall at each setting from 0.70 to 1.30, scored for fidelity (adjacent pairs found in the corpus) against novelty (completions not verbatim Shakespeare); since the engine learned to measure its own floor the curve is a broad plateau — level to within two points from 0.70 to 1.05, collapsing only past 1.1 — and 0.95 sits comfortably upon it. The press's 0.85: under the cams the drum is already governed by the harness, and the sheets themselves can be scored — thirty a configuration, on the concordance's own measure — where 0.85 buys some five points of quotation for no loss of rhyme. The rhymer judges by sound with the Bard’s own licences (love/prove, eyes/lies, past/waste), was made to pass 106 of his own rhyme-pairs before it was let judge the engine’s, and counts no word a rhyme against itself nor against its own possessive. The rhyme cam’s success at 5 · 10 · 20 sonnets and the whole folio, twenty sheets the tier: four in five, near enough, at every notch — it does not climb with the schooling, for a larger library brings more rhyme-fellows and more ways to wander both.
